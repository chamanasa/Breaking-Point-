Frameworks & Approach

Each card follows the same shape: what it is, when to use it and one worked example. The “when to use” column matters most, because picking the right tool is what separates a clean answer from a messy one.

Top-down vs bottom-up MECE Pareto (80/20) Unit economics Extrapolation Demand side Supply side & TAM-SAM-SOM

Top-down vs. bottom-up

What it is

Top-down starts from a large known total and narrows it with ratios. Bottom-up starts from the smallest countable unit (a household, a store, a seat) and builds up.

When to use it

Use top-down when a reliable big number exists (national sales, total population) and you need a quick share. Use bottom-up when the drivers are local and observable, or when the interviewer wants to see granular logic. Doing both is the strongest sanity check.

Worked example: number of private cars in Bengaluru

Top-down

India has ~30 cars per 1,000 people, so about 43 M cars. Bengaluru has ~1% of India’s people but far higher incomes, so assume ~5% of the cars.

43 M × 5% ≈ 2.2 M cars

Bottom-up

14 M people ÷ 4 per household = 3.5 M households.
High income 15% × 90% own × 1.5 cars = 0.7 M
Middle 45% × 40% own × 1 car = 0.63 M
Low 40% × 5% own × 1 car = 0.07 M
Add ~15% for cabs and fleets: ~1.6 M cars

The two answers are the same order of magnitude. Next, explain the gap: is Bengaluru’s 5% share too high, or is middle-income ownership too low?

MECE

What it is

Mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive. Split the problem into segments that don’t overlap (so nothing is counted twice) and that together cover everything (so nothing is missed).

When to use it

Every time you build a tree. Check each level of segmentation against both tests before you put numbers on it. The easiest checks are splits that add up by construction, such as age bands, urban/rural or buyer type.

Worked example: who buys edible oil in India?

Not MECE

“Vegetarian families, restaurants, North Indian households.” Vegetarian and North Indian households overlap, and food manufacturers are missing entirely.

MECE

By buyer type: households + HoReCa (hotels, restaurants, caterers) + food processors (snacks, bakeries, sweets). Every litre is bought by exactly one of these.

Pareto principle (80/20)

What it is

A small share of units (roughly 20%) often drives most of the outcome (roughly 80%). Model that heavy minority carefully and approximate everyone else with one average.

When to use it

When usage is highly skewed: trading, airline miles, restaurant revenue, B2B customers, social-media posting. It saves time in a time-boxed interview. Say the assumption out loud, since the interviewer may want the full split.

Worked example: daily trades on a broking app with 1 M active users

SegmentUsersTrades / dayTotal
Heavy traders (top 20%)200 k102.0 M
Everyone else800 k0.60.48 M
Total~2.5 M

The top 20% produce ~80% of trades, so the answer depends mostly on the “10 trades a day” assumption. That’s the one to defend.

Unit economics

What it is

Work out the economics of one unit (one order, customer, ride or seat), then multiply by volume and subtract fixed costs.

When to use it

For revenue, cost or profit questions about a single business: a café, cloud kitchen, delivery fleet or SaaS product. It also exposes whether the business makes money at all, which is often the follow-up question.

Worked example: monthly profit of a cloud kitchen

Average order value₹400
− Food cost (35%)₹140
− Aggregator commission (25%)₹100
− Packaging (5%)₹20
Contribution per order₹140
× 60 orders/day × 30 days₹2.52 L
− Fixed costs (rent ₹60 k, staff ₹1.2 L, utilities ₹20 k)₹2.0 L
Monthly profit~₹52 k

Extrapolation

What it is

Project a known current value forward at a steady growth rate.

Y = Y′ × (1 + r)t

Y = future value, Y′ = current known value, r = annual growth rate, t = number of years.

When to use it

For “in 2030” or “in five years” questions, where today’s figure is knowable and the trend is stable. State why the rate holds (policy, falling prices, adoption curve) and note where it might flatten.

Worked example: electric two-wheeler sales in 2030

~1.1 M sold this year, growing 25% a year for 5 years:

1.1 M × 1.255 = 1.1 M × 3.05 ≈ 3.4 M units

Shortcut: with the rule of 72, something growing at 25% doubles roughly every 72 ÷ 25 ≈ 3 years.

Demand-side approach

What it is

Size the market from the buyer’s side: who the target population is, how many of them use the product, how often, and how much each time.

Population × % users × frequency × quantity

When to use it

For market size, revenue and consumption questions where supply is abundant and the limit is how many people want it. Examples: sanitary pads in India, daily Delhi Metro riders, toothpaste sales.

Parameters to consider

  • Age
  • Income
  • Location (urban / rural, tier)
  • Gender
  • Occupation
  • Literacy
  • Mobile penetration
  • Internet penetration
  • Usage frequency
  • Household vs individual

Supply-side approach

What it is

Size the market from the seller’s side: production capacity, number of outlets or vehicles, throughput per unit of time, and utilisation.

Capacity × throughput × hours × utilisation

When to use it

When the business is supply-constrained: production capacity, delivery or fleet limits, seats and slots, regulation (licences, runway slots) or transport bottlenecks. Examples: Amazon parcels delivered per day, flights out of an airport, limited-edition sneakers resold.

Worked example: orders per day at a quick-commerce dark store

25 riders on shift × 16 operating hours × 3 deliveries per rider per hour (20-minute round trip) × 60% utilisation = ~720 orders a day.

Mini-framework: TAM · SAM · SOM

TAMTotal addressable market: everyone who could possibly buy.
SAMServiceable available market: the part of TAM your product, channel and geography can actually serve.
SOMServiceable obtainable market: the share of SAM you can realistically win, given competition and resources.

Example: an online-only CAT prep start-up. TAM = ~3.3 lakh CAT registrants × ₹20 k average spend on preparation = ₹660 Cr. SAM = the ~50% open to online-only coaching = ₹330 Cr. SOM = 5% share in three years = ~₹16.5 Cr.
